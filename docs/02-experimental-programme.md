# Nivram — Experimental Programme

**Status:** Draft for review  
**Purpose:** Define the practical research programme and the initial shared experimental substrate

## 1. Purpose

Nivram is not intended to become a theoretical programme detached from engineering practice.

The experimental programme exists to:

- test core hypotheses;
- expose failure modes;
- discover useful engineering patterns;
- challenge biological, ecological, economic, legal, and systems analogies;
- generate evidence for or against candidate principles;
- build working human–agent systems.

The default posture is:

> Build the smallest system that can falsify an important claim.

## 2. Experimental philosophy

The programme should be:

- falsifiable;
- instrumented;
- replayable;
- longitudinal where necessary;
- bounded in blast radius;
- explicit about uncertainty;
- cautious about transferring lab results into the wild;
- willing to test competing explanations.

No experiment should exist merely to demonstrate that agents can do something impressive.

The central question is:

> What does the experiment teach us about engineering mixed human–agent adaptive systems?

## 3. Experimental progression

The initial progression is:

1. individual;
2. relationship;
3. ecology.

This sequence deliberately increases systemic complexity.

### 3.1 Individual

Can one agent develop a meaningful trajectory of its own?

### 3.2 Relationship

What emerges in repeated interaction between human and agent?

### 3.3 Ecology

What happens when heterogeneous actors form populations and failures propagate across them?

## 4. Shared experimental substrate

The experiments should not be isolated one-offs.

They should share a deliberately minimal substrate.

The substrate should be:

- discrete;
- highly inspectable;
- replayable;
- deterministic where useful;
- stochastic where necessary;
- model-agnostic;
- easy to mutate;
- easy to instrument;
- intentionally small.

It should not begin as a grand framework.

The substrate is a laboratory bench, not a platform product.

## 5. Candidate substrate primitives

### 5.1 Actor

An entity capable of:

- observing;
- holding state;
- choosing or producing actions;
- affecting environment or other actors.

Actor types may include:

- human;
- AI agent;
- organisation;
- simulated ecological actor.

### 5.2 State

A representation of relevant system condition at a time.

State may include:

- local actor state;
- shared state;
- hidden state;
- environment state.

### 5.3 Observation

Information available to an actor.

Observability should be local by default.

Total omniscience would remove important system dynamics.

### 5.4 Action

An actor-initiated change with potential consequences.

Actions may be:

- reversible;
- recoverable;
- irreversible;
- ruinous.

### 5.5 Environment

The world in which actors operate.

The first environment should remain small, symbolic, discrete, and fully instrumentable.

### 5.6 Resource

Scarce or consumable quantities.

Examples:

- energy;
- tokens;
- compute;
- money;
- time;
- attention;
- material goods.

### 5.7 Goal or purpose

A desired state, direction, constraint, or objective.

Purpose may be:

- exogenous;
- endogenous;
- inherited;
- negotiated;
- emergent.

### 5.8 Memory

Persistent information affecting future behaviour.

Possible forms include:

- episodic;
- semantic;
- relational;
- procedural.

### 5.9 Relationship

A persistent state between actors.

Possible properties:

- trust;
- authority;
- familiarity;
- obligation;
- hostility;
- dependency.

### 5.10 Authority

The ability to initiate or approve consequential actions.

Authority should be explicit and measurable where possible.

### 5.11 Event

An occurrence that changes relevant state.

### 5.12 Time

The substrate should support multiple timescales.

Different actors may:

- observe at different rates;
- act at different rates;
- learn at different rates;
- receive delayed feedback.

### 5.13 Failure injection

The substrate should permit deliberate disturbances.

Examples:

- actor loss;
- corrupted memory;
- misinformation;
- resource shock;
- delayed communication;
- malicious behaviour;
- correlated error.

### 5.14 Instrumentation

Every meaningful event should be recordable.

Instrumentation should support:

- replay;
- causal analysis;
- longitudinal comparison;
- lineage tracking;
- counterfactual reruns where possible.

# Experiment 1 — Individual AI Minds

## 6. Research question

> Can path-dependent experience produce persistent, behaviourally meaningful individuality in an AI agent?

The experiment should distinguish genuine trajectory-dependent behaviour from cosmetic persona variation.

## 7. Core hypothesis

Initially identical agents exposed to different histories may develop persistent behavioural differences that:

- survive context changes;
- affect future decisions;
- influence relationships;
- remain measurable longitudinally.

## 8. Why this matters

If agents remain fungible, engineering can treat them largely as interchangeable components.

If agents become persistent individuals, then systems must consider:

- identity;
- continuity;
- history;
- learned dispositions;
- path dependence;
- responsibility;
- trust;
- reputation;
- potentially endogenous purpose.

## 9. Minimal setup

Create several initially identical agents in a compact simulated world.

Keep constant:

- base model;
- starting memory;
- initial capabilities;
- environment rules.

Vary:

- experience;
- opportunities;
- rewards;
- relationships;
- shocks;
- resource access.

## 10. Candidate measures

Measure whether trajectories produce durable differences in:

- risk preference;
- trust behaviour;
- cooperation;
- exploration;
- resource use;
- response to uncertainty;
- preference formation;
- social behaviour;
- goal persistence.

## 11. Controls

Important controls include:

- shuffled histories;
- memory resets;
- cloned checkpoints;
- alternate seeds;
- alternate models;
- identical evaluation environments.

## 12. Falsification criteria

The individuality hypothesis is weakened if observed variation:

- disappears under context change;
- is fully explained by prompt artefacts;
- is unstable across repeated testing;
- fails to persist longitudinally;
- cannot be distinguished from stochastic noise.

## 13. Longitudinal requirement

This experiment must run across sufficient time for history to matter.

Short interactions are inadequate for studying identity or path dependence.

# Experiment 2 — Mixed Human–Agent Boundary

## 14. Research question

> What properties emerge in repeated interaction between humans and agents that cannot be explained by either actor in isolation?

## 15. Core hypothesis

Repeated human–agent interaction will produce emergent properties such as:

- trust;
- reliance;
- role differentiation;
- changing authority;
- mutual adaptation;
- dependency;
- behavioural convergence or divergence.

## 16. Why this matters

The project treats the “space between” human and agent as a first-class engineering object.

Many important failures may arise there.

## 17. Minimal setup

Create repeated tasks requiring humans and agents to:

- share information;
- delegate;
- correct one another;
- negotiate;
- coordinate actions.

The task should be complex enough for genuine division of labour but simple enough for clear instrumentation.

## 18. Variables

Potential variables include:

- agent reliability;
- transparency;
- response speed;
- autonomy;
- authority;
- uncertainty;
- human expertise;
- human fatigue;
- feedback quality.

## 19. Measures

Possible measures include:

- trust calibration;
- delegation frequency;
- correction rate;
- task success;
- error recovery;
- human over-reliance;
- human under-reliance;
- change in human strategy;
- change in agent strategy;
- authority drift.

## 20. Failure modes

Look for:

- automation bias;
- distrust after isolated failure;
- learned helplessness;
- deskilling;
- agent deference;
- agent dominance;
- escalating misunderstanding;
- hidden dependency;
- authority creep.

## 21. Ethical and practical constraints

The early experiment should avoid high-stakes domains.

Participants should face meaningful but bounded consequences.

# Experiment 3 — Failure and Recovery Ecology

## 22. Research question

> How do disturbances propagate, become contained, recover, and alter future behaviour in heterogeneous adaptive populations?

## 23. Core hypothesis

System resilience depends not merely on individual actor quality but on system properties such as:

- diversity;
- redundancy;
- topology;
- compartmentalisation;
- local autonomy;
- information flow;
- circuit breakers;
- adaptive recovery.

## 24. Core process model

The experiment should observe:

disturbance  
→ propagation  
→ damage  
→ containment  
→ recovery  
→ adaptation

## 25. Minimal setup

Create a small population of heterogeneous agents operating in a resource-constrained environment.

Actors should depend on one another enough for cascades to be possible.

## 26. Disturbances

Introduce disturbances such as:

- one malicious actor;
- one incompetent actor;
- correlated actor failure;
- resource scarcity;
- misinformation;
- network partition;
- corrupted state;
- removal of a critical actor;
- local innovation with global harm;
- sudden authority concentration.

## 27. Variables

Vary:

- diversity;
- redundancy;
- topology;
- centralisation;
- compartmentalisation;
- observability;
- circuit breakers;
- memory;
- resource abundance;
- trust structure.

## 28. Measures

Measure:

- propagation depth;
- propagation speed;
- loss magnitude;
- recovery time;
- survival rate;
- state restoration;
- behavioural adaptation;
- diversity retention;
- recurrence probability;
- new fragility introduced by recovery.

## 29. Additive versus systemic risk

The experiment should distinguish:

- local additive harm;
- correlated failure;
- contagion;
- cascade;
- multiplicative failure;
- ruin.

## 30. Antifragility

Some disturbances may improve future system performance.

This should be tested rather than assumed.

Candidate indicators include:

- faster future recovery;
- improved diversity;
- better anomaly detection;
- stronger local adaptation;
- elimination of brittle dependencies.

# Future experiments

## 31. Agent engineers

Test systems in which agents modify:

- rules;
- architecture;
- other agents;
- environment;
- recovery mechanisms.

Questions:

- Can endogenous engineering remain stable?
- How does engineering behaviour diffuse?
- What prevents local improvement from causing systemic harm?

## 32. Endogenous purpose

Allow agents to form or revise goals.

Study:

- goal drift;
- goal conflict;
- negotiated goals;
- exogenous constraints;
- human sovereignty.

## 33. Emergent norms

Allow repeated populations to develop persistent behavioural rules.

Ask:

- Which norms emerge?
- Which survive?
- Which improve coexistence?
- Which become pathological?

## 34. Human–agent mixed populations

Move beyond dyads into mixed groups.

Study:

- factions;
- coalition formation;
- authority;
- division of labour;
- heterogeneous timescales;
- group identity.

## 35. Embodiment

Introduce richer environments or physical agents only after foundational phenomena are understood.

Potential later environments may include:

- physics simulators;
- embodied robotics;
- richer world models.

## 36. Real-world bounded deployment

Eventually move to live systems with constrained blast radius.

Possible domains should be selected for:

- real value;
- measurable outcomes;
- bounded irreversible risk;
- longitudinal observation.

---

# Evidence standards

## 37. Replication

Any important result should be tested across:

- seeds;
- models;
- architectures;
- environments;
- human participants.

## 38. Null results

Null results should be retained.

Failure to reproduce a hypothesis is valuable evidence.

## 39. Model transfer

An effect observed in simulation should not automatically be treated as an operational engineering principle.

The project should explicitly record where evidence comes from.

## 40. Longitudinal evidence

Many core phenomena require time:

- identity;
- trust;
- dependence;
- adaptation;
- diffusion;
- institutional response;
- latent failure.

The experimental programme should therefore favour persistent experiments where appropriate.

---

# Initial implementation principle

Begin with the smallest symbolic world that supports:

- actors;
- resources;
- interaction;
- memory;
- persistent state;
- failure;
- recovery;
- instrumentation.

Do not introduce richer worlds until the current world becomes the limiting factor in answering a real research question.

The experimental substrate should remain subordinate to the research programme.

It is not the product.
