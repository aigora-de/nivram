# Nivram — Holding Pen for Deferred Project Documents

**Status:** Working draft / holding pen  
**Purpose:** Preserve and organise material intended for later extraction into five standalone documents without prematurely freezing their structure.

This document contains the remaining project material that does not yet warrant a fully independent artefact.

The five intended future documents are:

1. Failure Modes and Evaluation Handbook
2. Human–Agent Field Guide
3. Canonical Concepts and Vocabulary
4. Minimal Experimental Substrate
5. Project Roadmap

The material below should be treated as provisional. Sections may later be promoted, merged, rewritten, or discarded as the experimental programme develops.

# 1. Failure Modes and Evaluation Handbook

## 1.1 Purpose

This document should eventually become a practical handbook for evaluating mixed human–agent systems and reasoning about failure, recovery, resilience, and systemic risk.

The central principle is:

> Failure and recovery should be analysed together.

A mature human–agent system should not be judged only by whether failures occur, but by how failures:

- arise;
- propagate;
- become visible;
- remain hidden;
- are contained;
- are recovered from;
- alter future behaviour;
- strengthen or weaken adaptive capacity.

The handbook should bridge:

- design;
- experimentation;
- operations;
- incident analysis;
- risk assessment;
- longitudinal evaluation.

## 1.2 Start with “good”

Failure has no useful meaning without some working notion of desirable behaviour.

A practical framing is:

### Good

The system supports desirable functioning, adaptation, coexistence, or flourishing within the relevant context.

### Bad

The system produces undesirable consequences but remains recoverable.

### Ugly

The system crosses into states that are:

- irreversible;
- ruinous;
- survival-threatening;
- or prohibitively difficult to recover from.

The distinction between bad and ugly is therefore strongly related to:

- reversibility;
- recoverability;
- optionality;
- survivability;
- ruin.

## 1.3 Contested failure

Failure may be relative rather than absolute.

One actor’s failure may be another actor’s success.

Examples include:

- markets;
- negotiations;
- competition;
- conflict;
- ecological displacement;
- institutional reform.

Any evaluation should therefore ask:

- failure for whom?
- success for whom?
- at what scale?
- over what time horizon?
- against which value system?
- according to which purpose?

## 1.4 Failure taxonomy

A layered taxonomy should distinguish at least the following.

### Actor or component failure

Examples:

- incorrect inference;
- hallucination;
- bad human judgement;
- malicious behaviour;
- resource exhaustion;
- memory corruption;
- policy violation;
- inability to act.

### Interaction failure

Examples:

- miscommunication;
- mistrust;
- excessive trust;
- authority confusion;
- delegation failure;
- coordination breakdown;
- misunderstanding;
- dependency;
- manipulation.

### Hidden or latent failure

Examples:

- silent degradation;
- accumulated dependency;
- loss of diversity;
- deskilling;
- hidden model drift;
- erosion of trust;
- weakening of independent human capability;
- increasing concentration of authority.

These may remain invisible until a trigger exposes them.

### Emergent failure

Examples:

- collective behaviour not present in any single actor;
- runaway coordination;
- collusion;
- herding;
- monoculture;
- pathological equilibrium;
- collective stupidity;
- bad attractors.

### Systemic failure

Examples:

- correlated failure;
- contagion;
- cascade;
- cross-domain propagation;
- multiplicative risk;
- infrastructure collapse.

### Evolutionary failure

Examples:

- maladaptive selection;
- harmful norms becoming dominant;
- loss of requisite variety;
- local adaptations that damage system viability;
- competitive dynamics favouring dangerous actors.

### Civilisational or ecological failure

Examples:

- irreversible degradation of foundational institutions;
- loss of meaningful human sovereignty;
- biosphere-level harms;
- civilisation-scale cascade;
- systemic resource depletion.

## 1.5 Symptoms versus causes

Observed failure may be remote from underlying cause.

The handbook should distinguish:

- trigger;
- proximate mechanism;
- enabling conditions;
- propagation path;
- latent structural cause;
- visible symptom;
- downstream consequence.

This is especially important in complex adaptive systems where the first visible failure may occur far from the causal origin.

## 1.6 Unknown unknowns

The handbook should explicitly account for:

- known knowns;
- known unknowns;
- unknown unknowns;
- unintended consequences;
- unseen emergent properties.

No risk method should imply complete observability or complete model coverage.

## 1.7 Scale

Risk should be analysed at multiple scales.

A useful ladder is:

individual  
→ pair / team / family  
→ organisation  
→ community  
→ infrastructure / ecosystem  
→ state  
→ civilisation  
→ biosphere

A failure can be:

- tolerable locally but damaging systemically;
- catastrophic locally but negligible globally;
- beneficial at one scale and harmful at another.

## 1.8 Additive versus systemic risk

Important classes include:

### Additive risk

Failures accumulate but remain largely independent and local.

### Correlated risk

Many actors fail simultaneously because of a shared dependency or common cause.

### Contagious risk

Failure propagates actor-to-actor.

### Cascading risk

Failure crosses layers, subsystems, or domains.

### Multiplicative risk

Two or more risks amplify one another.

### Ruin risk

A failure crosses a boundary beyond which meaningful recovery is unavailable.

## 1.9 Resilience vocabulary

The handbook should distinguish:

### Robustness

Resistance to disturbance.

### Resilience

Ability to recover after disturbance.

### Recoverability

Ability to restore acceptable functioning.

### Survivability

Ability to remain viable despite severe disturbance.

### Antifragility

Ability to improve because of some classes of disturbance.

### Ruin avoidance

Prevention of irreversible destruction of the system or critical parts of it.

These terms should not be treated as synonyms.

## 1.10 Recovery mechanisms

Candidate mechanisms include:

- circuit breakers;
- containment;
- compartmentalisation;
- redundancy;
- heterogeneity;
- diversity;
- graceful degradation;
- fallback modes;
- immune-like detection;
- anomaly detection;
- role substitution;
- rollback where possible;
- isolation;
- quarantine;
- resource throttling;
- revocation of authority;
- local sacrifice to preserve wider viability.

## 1.11 Antifragility and useful failure

Some failures may be:

- tolerated;
- deliberately induced;
- exploratory;
- useful.

A system may benefit if local failures:

- expose fragility;
- trigger adaptation;
- improve detection;
- eliminate brittle dependencies;
- increase future recovery capacity.

The handbook should avoid the naive principle that fewer failures always means a better system.

## 1.12 Evaluation criteria

Evaluation should not be restricted to task success.

Candidate dimensions include:

- task performance;
- human agency;
- agent autonomy;
- adaptability;
- diversity;
- resilience;
- recoverability;
- systemic stability;
- trust calibration;
- authority distribution;
- contestability;
- ecological impact;
- resource use;
- innovation capacity;
- continued adaptive capacity;
- preservation of optionality;
- exposure to ruin.

These may be quantitative or qualitative.

## 1.13 Evaluation as longitudinal practice

Some important properties only become visible over time:

- dependency;
- deskilling;
- trust;
- adaptation;
- goal drift;
- institutional lag;
- correlated fragility;
- emergent norms;
- identity;
- power concentration.

Evaluation should therefore include longitudinal observation rather than one-shot benchmarking.

# 2. Human–Agent Field Guide

## 2.1 Purpose

The field guide should become the practical companion for engineers, operators, researchers, and decision-makers working with mixed human–agent systems.

It should help answer:

> What should I look for, ask, test, monitor, and intervene on?

It should not become a bureaucratic checklist.

Instead, it should offer compact heuristics and prompts for thinking.

## 2.2 System scoping

Ask:

- What is the system of interest here?
- Why is that boundary useful?
- What important causal factors sit just outside it?
- At what scale are we reasoning?
- Over what time horizon?
- What changes if the boundary is widened or narrowed?

## 2.3 Actor discovery

Identify:

- humans;
- agents;
- teams;
- organisations;
- institutions;
- markets;
- ecological or environmental actors;
- infrastructure dependencies.

Then ask:

- Which actors can act?
- Which can refuse?
- Which can adapt?
- Which can learn?
- Which can alter others?
- Which can reproduce or create successors?
- Which carry responsibility?
- Which bear consequences?

## 2.4 Purpose and interest

Ask:

- What is the declared system purpose?
- Who declared it?
- Is there an exogenous purpose reference?
- What local goals exist?
- Which purposes are endogenous?
- Which purposes conflict?
- Which actors benefit?
- Which actors bear costs?
- What incentives exist?
- Where is skin-in-the-game absent?

## 2.5 Authority

Ask:

- Who can act?
- Who can approve?
- Who can revoke?
- Who can override?
- Is authority explicit?
- Can authority drift?
- Can effective authority diverge from formal authority?
- Can humans meaningfully intervene at agentic timescales?

## 2.6 Time

Ask:

- What are the characteristic timescales of each actor?
- How quickly can each sense?
- decide?
- act?
- learn?
- adapt?
- regulate?
- recover?

Look for dangerous timescale mismatches.

## 2.7 Observability

Ask:

- What can each actor observe?
- What remains hidden?
- What signals are delayed?
- What information cannot realistically be processed in time?
- Does increased observability actually improve outcomes?
- Are observers overloaded?

Avoid assuming global observability is possible or desirable.

## 2.8 Heterogeneity and diversity

Ask:

- Where is the system homogeneous?
- Does it rely on one model family?
- one data source?
- one policy?
- one architecture?
- one authority structure?
- one worldview?

Then ask whether common-mode failure is possible.

## 2.9 Irreversibility

Ask:

- Which actions are reversible?
- Which are recoverable?
- Which are irreversible?
- Which create ruin risk?
- Where is the point of no return?
- What must be known before commitment?
- What should remain optional longer?

## 2.10 Failure and recovery

Ask:

- What does failure mean here?
- What does recovery mean?
- Can the system fail locally without failing globally?
- What is hidden?
- What can cascade?
- What can spread by contagion?
- What can create correlated failure?
- What circuit breakers exist?
- What happens after recovery?

## 2.11 Emergence

Ask:

- What important properties arise only through interaction?
- What norms are emerging?
- What coalitions are forming?
- What dependencies are appearing?
- What unintended equilibria are forming?
- Are we moving toward an undesirable attractor?

## 2.12 Humans

Ask:

- Are humans adapting to the system?
- becoming dependent?
- becoming deskilled?
- becoming overconfident?
- becoming distrustful?
- becoming manipulated?
- changing social norms?
- offloading moral responsibility?

Humans should not be modelled as static supervisors.

## 2.13 Agent individuality

Ask:

- Are agents still fungible?
- Do they have persistent histories?
- relationships?
- reputations?
- learned dispositions?
- identity?
- endogenous preferences?

If not fungible, replacement assumptions may be invalid.

## 2.14 Ecological dependencies

Ask:

- What resources sustain the system?
- energy?
- water?
- compute?
- capital?
- minerals?
- human attention?
- land?
- infrastructure?

What other systems compete for the same resources?

## 2.15 Value and goodness

Ask:

- Good for whom?
- Over what timescale?
- At what scale?
- According to which moral, economic, ecological, or cultural frame?
- What is being optimised?
- What is deliberately not being optimised?

## 2.16 Evidence

Ask:

- What evidence supports this assumption?
- Is it simulation evidence?
- lab evidence?
- field evidence?
- longitudinal evidence?
- has it replicated?
- what would falsify it?
- where does the evidence stop applying?

## 2.17 Intervention

Before intervention, ask:

- Is intervention necessary?
- What happens if we do nothing?
- Can we intervene locally?
- What second-order effects may result?
- Can the intervention itself diffuse?
- Can it create new fragility?
- How will we know whether it worked?

# 3. Canonical Concepts and Vocabulary

## 3.1 Purpose

The project needs a common vocabulary, but not a maximal ontology.

Terms should be stabilised only where they materially improve:

- engineering;
- experimentation;
- communication;
- reasoning;
- reproducibility.

Definitions should remain revisable.

## 3.2 Actor

An entity capable of participating causally in the system.

Depending on context, an actor may:

- observe;
- infer;
- choose;
- act;
- adapt;
- affect other actors;
- hold goals or purposes.

Possible actor classes include:

- human;
- artificial agent;
- organisation;
- institution;
- simulated biological actor.

## 3.3 Agent

An actor with some capacity to:

- sense or observe;
- maintain state;
- infer;
- select actions;
- act toward goals or purposes.

Agency is likely a matter of degree rather than binary classification.

## 3.4 Human

A biological human actor.

Humans should not be reduced automatically to a generic actor because properties such as embodiment, mortality, legal standing, moral responsibility, identity, and cultural meaning may matter.

## 3.5 Organisation

A persistent collective actor with:

- roles;
- rules;
- identity;
- memory;
- authority;
- incentives;
- internal structure.

## 3.6 System of Interest

The system boundary selected for a particular engineering or research purpose.

The SOI is:

- contextual;
- local;
- revisable;
- purpose-dependent.

## 3.7 Environment

That which is relevant to the system but not represented as part of the current SOI.

Because boundaries are contextual, something may be environment in one analysis and part of the SOI in another.

## 3.8 State

A representation of relevant system condition at a time.

State may be:

- local;
- shared;
- hidden;
- inferred;
- partial.

## 3.9 Observation

Information available to an actor about itself, other actors, or the environment.

Observation is not assumed to be complete or correct.

## 3.10 Inference

The process by which an actor derives beliefs, predictions, explanations, or expectations from available information.

## 3.11 Decision

A selection among possible actions, policies, commitments, or interpretations.

A decision may be explicit or implicit.

## 3.12 Action

A causal intervention by an actor that changes or attempts to change system state.

Actions may differ in:

- scope;
- authority;
- reversibility;
- consequence;
- observability.

## 3.13 Event

A meaningful occurrence associated with state change.

Events may be:

- exogenous;
- endogenous;
- instantaneous;
- observed;
- hidden.

## 3.14 Activity / Process

A temporally extended sequence of actions or changes.

## 3.15 Goal

A desired state, region, condition, or direction.

Goals may have:

- prerequisites;
- dependencies;
- acceptance criteria;
- success criteria.

## 3.16 Purpose

The broader reason or end toward which an actor, system, or activity is oriented.

Purpose may be:

- exogenous;
- endogenous;
- inherited;
- negotiated;
- emergent;
- contested.

## 3.17 Authority

Legitimate or effective permission to make decisions or cause consequential action.

Formal authority and effective authority may differ.

## 3.18 Autonomy

The degree to which an actor can choose and act without immediate external instruction.

Autonomy should not be conflated with sovereignty.

## 3.19 Sovereignty

Ultimate recognised authority within a relevant domain.

Human sovereignty is a possible normative constraint, not an assumption to discard.

## 3.20 Relationship

Persistent state between actors arising from interaction.

Examples:

- trust;
- obligation;
- dependence;
- hostility;
- familiarity;
- authority.

## 3.21 NOT-SELF / OTHER-MODEL

An actor’s representation of other actors.

Potential contents include:

- identity;
- capability;
- preference;
- trustworthiness;
- likely behaviour.

## 3.22 ENVIRONMENT-MODEL

An actor’s representation of the environment.

This representation is likely to be:

- partial;
- local;
- uncertain;
- dynamic.

## 3.23 Emergence

System behaviour or properties that arise from interactions and are not usefully attributable to one component alone.

## 3.24 Path dependence

Dependence of current behaviour or state on historical trajectory.

## 3.25 Attractor

A state or region of system behaviour toward which dynamics tend.

## 3.26 Basin

A region of state space from which the system tends toward a particular attractor.

## 3.27 Failure

A condition judged undesirable relative to some actor, purpose, scale, time horizon, or value framework.

Failure may be contested.

## 3.28 Recovery

Restoration or reconfiguration toward viable or acceptable functioning after disturbance.

## 3.29 Robustness

Ability to resist disturbance.

## 3.30 Resilience

Ability to recover after disturbance.

## 3.31 Antifragility

Ability to improve through exposure to some classes of stress or failure.

## 3.32 Ruin

Irreversible or effectively irreversible loss of viability at a relevant scale.

## 3.33 Coexistence

The condition in which heterogeneous purposive actors continue to share a system despite differing or conflicting goals.

## 3.34 Alignment

A relationship in which actor behaviour conforms sufficiently to another actor’s goals, constraints, or values.

Alignment is useful but not sufficient as the overall framing for mixed human–agent systems.

## 3.35 Ecology

A system of interacting populations and environments involving:

- competition;
- cooperation;
- resources;
- adaptation;
- niches;
- selection;
- flows.

Whether human–agent systems are literally ecological systems or merely well-modelled by ecological concepts remains an empirical question.

# 4. Minimal Experimental Substrate

## 4.1 Purpose

The substrate should support the experimental programme without becoming a large framework.

It is intended as a reusable laboratory for:

- agent individuality;
- human–agent interaction;
- failure and recovery;
- population dynamics;
- later mixed human–agent ecologies.

The substrate should be deliberately easy to discard or restructure.

## 4.2 Design principles

The first substrate should be:

- small;
- discrete;
- symbolic;
- highly instrumented;
- replayable;
- inspectable;
- mutable;
- model-agnostic;
- local-observation friendly;
- able to support multiple timescales.

## 4.3 Core primitives

The initial implementation may need:

- actor;
- actor type;
- state;
- environment;
- resource;
- observation;
- action;
- goal;
- memory;
- relationship;
- authority;
- event;
- time;
- communication;
- failure injection;
- instrumentation.

Lineage and reproduction can be added later.

## 4.4 World model

Start with a small discrete world.

Avoid early use of:

- rich 3D simulation;
- complex physics;
- embodiment;
- large open environments.

These add uncontrolled complexity before the project knows which phenomena matter.

## 4.5 Local observability

Actors should not automatically see global system state.

Local observability allows experiments involving:

- uncertainty;
- misinformation;
- hidden failure;
- asymmetric knowledge;
- trust;
- inference.

## 4.6 Multiple clocks

Different actors should be able to operate at different frequencies.

This supports experimentation with heterogeneous time.

Possible actor clocks:

- observation rate;
- decision rate;
- action rate;
- learning rate;
- institutional response rate.

## 4.7 Persistent memory

Memory must support longitudinal experiments.

Candidate forms include:

- episodic memory;
- semantic memory;
- relational memory;
- procedural memory.

## 4.8 Actor replacement and identity

The substrate should allow:

- cloning;
- checkpointing;
- resetting;
- replacement;
- history transfer;
- memory deletion.

This enables testing of fungibility and individuality.

## 4.9 Resources

Include at least one scarce resource.

This allows study of:

- incentives;
- cooperation;
- competition;
- scarcity;
- trade;
- allocation;
- systemic stress.

## 4.10 Authority model

Actions should be associated with permissions.

The substrate should allow:

- delegation;
- revocation;
- escalation;
- override;
- authority drift experiments.

## 4.11 Failure injection

Provide hooks to inject:

- actor failure;
- malicious behaviour;
- corrupted memory;
- false information;
- resource shocks;
- delayed communication;
- correlated error;
- network partition.

## 4.12 Replay and observability

The substrate should record enough information to reconstruct an experiment.

Useful logs include:

- actor state;
- action;
- observation;
- communication;
- memory updates;
- environmental state;
- authority change;
- resource state;
- failures;
- recovery events.

## 4.13 Counterfactuals

Where practical, experiments should support reruns from checkpoints with:

- changed seeds;
- changed actors;
- changed models;
- changed failures;
- changed rules.

## 4.14 Human participation

Human interaction can initially occur through simple interfaces.

The substrate should not require sophisticated UI.

The aim is to study interaction dynamics, not interface polish.

## 4.15 What not to build yet

Do not initially build:

- a general-purpose agent platform;
- a distributed production runtime;
- a complex ontology service;
- an enterprise UI;
- elaborate governance infrastructure;
- realistic 3D worlds;
- embodied robotics.

These may come later only if demanded by experiments.

# 5. Project Roadmap

## 5.1 Purpose

The roadmap should coordinate conceptual work, experiments, engineering, and evidence without turning the project into a waterfall programme.

The phases below indicate emphasis, not hard stage gates.

## Phase 0 — Consolidation

### Objectives

- stabilise current synthesis;
- identify open questions;
- define initial vocabulary;
- establish hypothesis register;
- design minimal experimental substrate.

### Outputs

- project overview;
- main synthesis;
- experimental programme;
- hypothesis register;
- holding-pen material;
- minimal substrate design.

## Phase 1 — Individual

### Objective

Investigate whether persistent agent individuality emerges from path-dependent experience.

### Key questions

- Do agents diverge meaningfully?
- Is behaviour persistent?
- Are agents fungible?
- Does identity matter?
- Can endogenous preference or purpose emerge?

### Outputs

- experiment design;
- implementation;
- results;
- replication;
- revised theory.

## Phase 2 — Relationship

### Objective

Study the “space between” human and agent.

### Key questions

- How does trust emerge?
- How does authority drift?
- How do humans adapt?
- How do agents adapt?
- What produces dependency?
- What produces healthy complementarity?

### Outputs

- mixed human–agent experiments;
- interaction failure taxonomy;
- early practical design patterns.

## Phase 3 — Population

### Objective

Move from dyads to multi-actor ecologies.

### Key questions

- How does heterogeneity affect resilience?
- How do failures propagate?
- What creates cascades?
- What reduces common-mode failure?
- How do norms emerge?
- What role do circuit breakers play?

### Outputs

- failure-and-recovery ecology experiments;
- population-level risk models;
- recovery patterns.

## Phase 4 — Mixed Ecology

### Objective

Introduce:

- humans;
- multiple agents;
- organisations;
- institutional constraints;
- environmental constraints.

### Key questions

- How do mixed communities evolve?
- How do purposes conflict?
- How does power distribute?
- How do norms and institutions adapt?
- How do environmental constraints shape behaviour?

## Phase 5 — Live Bounded Systems

### Objective

Move into real-world use cases with limited blast radius.

### Selection criteria

Choose use cases that provide:

- real value;
- measurable consequences;
- bounded irreversible risk;
- observable adaptation;
- useful longitudinal data.

### Key questions

- Which simulated results survive in the wild?
- What new phenomena appear?
- Which models fail?
- Which engineering patterns remain useful?

## Phase 6 — Operational Discipline

### Objective

Turn surviving evidence into:

- engineering methods;
- design patterns;
- operational practices;
- evaluation methods;
- incident methods;
- governance/coexistence mechanisms.

The discipline should remain provisional.

## Phase 7 — Mature Field

### Objective

Develop a body of:

- replicated results;
- long-lived principles;
- reusable tools;
- working systems;
- accumulated incident knowledge;
- trained practitioners.

## 5.2 Workstreams operating across all phases

Some work should continue throughout the roadmap.

### Evidence

Continuously update:

- confidence;
- falsification status;
- replication status;
- scope.

### Vocabulary

Revise terms when experiments reveal weaknesses.

### Failure taxonomy

Expand from observed incidents and experiments.

### Normativity

Keep value questions explicit as system stakes increase.

### Ecological scope

Progressively include resource and environmental consequences.

### Human modelling

Add human properties only where justified by evidence and need.

## 5.3 Sequence of experimental environments

A useful progression is:

small symbolic world  
→ richer synthetic ecology  
→ human–agent laboratory  
→ bounded live system  
→ longitudinal operational system

The progression should not be automatic.

Move to the next environment only when the current one becomes the limiting factor.

## 5.4 Evidence maturity

Claims should mature approximately as follows:

hypothesis  
→ observation  
→ controlled evidence  
→ replicated evidence  
→ field evidence  
→ longitudinal field evidence  
→ provisional pattern  
→ repeatedly surviving engineering principle

No claim reaches a final immutable state.

## 5.5 Practical success criteria

The project is succeeding if it increasingly enables us to:

- build useful mixed human–agent systems;
- predict important failure modes;
- recover from failures;
- preserve optionality;
- avoid ruin;
- exploit beneficial adaptation;
- reason explicitly about scale and time;
- maintain meaningful human participation;
- distinguish genuine novelty from rebranded existing engineering knowledge;
- produce evidence that survives replication.

## 5.6 Long-term outcome

A mature Nivram project should leave behind more than software or papers.

It should provide a practical systems-engineering discipline for mixed human–machine adaptive systems, supported by:

- experiments;
- working systems;
- observed failures;
- recovery experience;
- reusable engineering methods;
- a living body of evidence.

The discipline should remain open to revision because the systems it studies are themselves evolving.
